# Toward a Craftsman Model
For some time now, I have been teaching students, primarily young people, how to make videos. Some come to class well already versed in storycraft, while others struggle to overcome the hurdle of pressing record or moving the camera nearer to their subject. Reflectively, I ask myself what can I share with the next generation that is pertinent to both of these groups? As I spend more time in my workshop and my basement making things out of wood and restoring my century-old home, I believe there are lessons to be learned in looking back at the historical and cultural decisions that have framed the development of moving pictures. What the revitalization of the craftsman movement represents in architecture, design, and furniture making; I believe that as we cross into a reality in which a high degree of media proficiency and literacy is no longer an exception but an expectation — our students must become craftsman. They must learn to hone their raw talents through a mastery of form: only then may they sharpen themselves into masters of the trade*.

*It should be noted that in the technical and precise definition: that hone and sharpen are not synonyms, and that honing is only the first step in preparing a cutting tool for use. 

## An Applied Art
Among the lasting impressions given to me during my time as a naïve and often careless undergraduate student were ones that came from my Production professor, Jim Krause. Jim is a seasoned veteran of public television, and maintained a working practice as a filmmaker while teaching the classes that shaped the backbone of my time at Indiana University. One of the truisms he frequently reiterated was that film and video was an "applied art" somewhere between the sciences and fine arts. At the time, IU had a fragmented approach to instructing the fields related to moving picture; subdivided into Telecommunications, Fine Arts, and Communications and Culture. As a Telecommunications student, our focus was often times laser focused on the "how" of filmmaking, and was primarily comprised of practical lab classes where we would learn to edit video or build motion graphics in large classrooms by way of the projector screen. If we were the "how", Fine Arts may have been the "what" and Communications and Culture the "why". It strikes me now as a relic of a closing era, where someone could have the good fortune of working in media, but only have to understand one facet of its construction.

It is no surprise that shortly after I departed IU, and the online media revolution was in full swing, that the filmmaking departments were reorganized as one holistic "Media Arts and Sciences" program.

## Honing
In a world where TikTok now has more than 1.8 billion users and thousands of videos are posted every second; the excuse that basic filmmaking is a skill that exceeds the ability of the lay person is not simply wrong, but laughable. The democratization of filmmaking tools: now carried daily with people around the globe in their pockets is a cataclysmic rebalancing of power. Much like the revolutions of the printing press and the personal computer, the effects of this change are dramatic and swift. At the same time we are swimming in a glut of low quality, vapid, and often unsatisfying visual media. These trends in communications technology are cyclical and predictable as with every new democratizing technological advancement from the telegram to the newspaper. 

However, what has not yet fully occurred within the relatively young medium of moving picture is the codification of grammar, syntax and formatting. We are living in a rough hewn time. In much the same way that the mechanics of the written word have morphed through popular forms from epic to novel and countless forms in between — the one-hour, thirty minute feature-film has only just begun to see its offspring take shape. What must occur now is a honing of these rough shapes into didactic forms; codified in the image of other pedagogical monoliths like the five-paragraph essay, the rule of thirds, and the haiku.
