# Stock Scenery Construction Updates for 2026
For the last five years, I have been working with my school's theater director to build scenery for our school plays.
In order to conserve resources and build a library of stock scenery I have been turning to [https://broadwaypress.com/bookpages/stockscenery.html | Bill Raoul's Stock Scenery Construction Handbook]. It has proven to be 
an invaluable source of technical know-how for the uninitiated. Below I will attempt to outline what my research has discovered. I welcome any criticism from Theater professionals who would publicly share their knowledge..

However, in the time since first publication (1990 as far as I can tell) there have been few changes made to account for the limited availability of the materials referenced in the book.

One of the sources of frustration in trying to build a set from stock broadway flats is the limited availability of lashing hardware, or a detailed description
of how to permanently or semi-permanently attach flats without this hardware. One of the hardware pieces frequently referenced is the "stage brace" which is no longer stocked by any web suppliers of theatrical equipment,
and many of the cleats referenced in the text are no longer in production as of this writing.

What I am attempting to document in this post is how to obtain materials and some of the "missing details" of flat scenery construction.

The knowledge here comes from IATSE or college theater programs that have documented some of these elements online and attempts to synthesize them for volunteer/amateur/high-school community theater programs.

# Using Jacks
{{< figure src="/images/setdesign/BroadwayJack.png" title="A sample Broadway jack" caption="These dimensions are all tied to our uniquely tall flats and should not not be used as a rule" width="50%" >}}

A noteworthy hole in the Raoul's Handbook is the use of jacks for holding up Broadway flats. The solution for holding up Broadway flats in Raoul's book are stage braces. When looking up stage braces online in 2026, they are listed as "near obsolete hardware". Pictures, videos and anecdotes online all point to 
using jacks. For the sake of simplicity I will outline what I will call "Broadway" jacks that are appropriately weighted for Broadway flats.

The stile on a jack should extend 2/3rds up the back of your flat. If the flat is on a platform, add the platform height to this measurement. The rail of the jack should be 1/4 the total height of the total vertical height.
Using CAD software can quickly calculate the miter angles — but you will likely need a compound miter box/scarf jig to cut these steep angles. Be thoughtful in the alignment of the grain in your corner bracing. 

# Attaching Jacks
There is little guidance on how to anchor flats in permanent locations from the Raoul Handbook. Here is what I have learned:
* Use [https://www.rosebrand.com/rose-brand-tight-pin-hinge?srsltid=AfmBOooeJpJm-eiibjTDP5DgWU9wirNdrB45ZehFe5m_mb7csL-8rFgS | 2" hinges] to anchor jacks to flats, and flats to the floor.
* Use screws that are 3/4s of the combined material thickness. (Hex Driven for easy removal.)

# Screws
While on the subject of screws, it is also worth reiterating to the newly-minted stagecraft practitioner that you should use construction screws instead of deck screws. The difference (other than price)
is that the construction screws are designed to handle shear forces better than deck screws. Never use drywall screws.

# Stiffening Flats
Use a stiffener on end in order to keep multiple flats in-line with one another! This is outlined on the Raoul book, but only in passing and this informations is not widely circulated outside of the greater tech theater web. 

Some Canadian resources refer to this stock material as a whaler. When used in other applications this construction is called hogs-trough. Searches for a combination of these three terms will help provide more clarity. [ https://www.ia470.com/primer/scenery.htm | This primer from IATSE has been invaluable].

# Sourcing Lumber in Big Box land.
We do not have a Mom and Pop lumberyard in my neck of the woods, which means making due with national-chain lumber suppliers. I try to buy 6" or 12" wide stock, as ripping this down
on the table saw seems to be the best bet for getting high quality, straight, knot free lumber, especially when building platform legs or load bearing structures.

{{< figure src="/images/setdesign/RippingStock.png" title="How to make 1\"x3\" from 12\" stock" caption="These dimensions are all tied to our uniquely tall flats and should not not be used as a rule" width="50%" >}}


Our local 84 Lumber seems to be able to order in a lot of the stock that we request, but has about a 1-2 week lead out time on 5/4"x and 1"x stock.

Lowes in our area sells 1"x12"x12' stock, but the quality can be hit or miss. It can be worth digging in a pinch to find boards without knots, but prices are sometimes $10 more than sourcing from 84.

# Casters, Wagon Brakes
Shop [https://www.mcmaster.com/ | McMaster Carr]. Much cheaper than "dedicated" theater webstores. [https://www.mcmaster.com/products/wagon-brake-clamps/mount-type~screw-on/ | Wagon Brakes], [https://www.mcmaster.com/products/casters/ | Casters]

# Sourcing Metal
Our local steel supplier is dramatically cheaper than McMaster for 3/16"x1"x5" stock, in 2025 it is less than 1/4 the price of online stores. Conduct a local web search of a bulk steel supplier in your area.
